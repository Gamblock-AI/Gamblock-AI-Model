Bad Blood: Taylor Swift Fan’s Suit Reveals Ticket-Holder Limits

April 9, 2026 9:40am
  • Facebook
  • Twitter
  • Google-preferred
  • Linkedin
  • + additional share options added

Buying a ticket to a game or concert might not seem like entering a contract—but a ticket is a contract, and it contains many terms and conditions, often including an arbitration provision.

That is key to a decision issued by a federal judge in Washington this week to compel arbitration in a dispute between a Taylor Swift fan and StubHub. While this dispute centers on tickets for a concert, the same basic principles apply for sports, and the same risks are true for fans who buy tickets to sporting events.

Last year, Alexis Christensen sued StubHub for fraud, misrepresentation, unjust enrichment and violation of consumer protection law, and the plaintiff demanded a jury trial. The lawsuit centers on a buying experience that left the customer feeling ripped off.

Christensen created a StubHub account in 2018 and, in August 2024, paid approximately $14,000 for three tickets—each ticket was worth approximately $4,670—for seats with an unobstructed, direct view of the stage for a Taylor Swift Eras Tour concert at BC Place Stadium in Vancouver, Canada, in December 2024. This was the final stop on Swift’s Eras Tour, which spanned 149 shows across five continents.

On the day of the concert, StubHub informed Christensen that the seats she purchased weren’t available, and she was instead offered replacement seats that were worth about $1,200 each—a total of $3,600. These seats were, according to Christensen, “plainly inferior,” and “provided a sharply angled side view of the stage.” She wasn’t refunded the difference and claims that StubHub betrayed its “FanProtect Guarantee,” which promises that the tickets bought or comparable ones will be delivered on time or customers receive a refund.

Christensen seeks to have her case certified as a class action on behalf of other StubHub customers who believe they have been wrongly treated. One of the proposed classes is a nationwide one, representing “all persons who purchased one or more tickets for an event through StubHub in the United States, within the applicable statute of limitations period.”

U.S. District Judge Jamal Whitehead agreed with StubHub that Christensen’s case can’t advance because she contractually agreed to arbitrate disputes when she bought the tickets from StubHub.

Whitehead reasoned that Christensen was provided clear notice of the terms and conditions, and that she accepted them.

He pointed out that directly beneath “sign in” buttons is language saying a user “agrees to our user agreement.” This warning also appears before a user buys a ticket, and hyperlinks can be clicked to detail the user agreement.

Whitehead further noted that StubHub’s user agreement tries to alert users of the arbitration provision. In fact, the first page of the agreement states, in bolded, all-capitalized text, that the “AGREEMENT CONTAINS AN AGREEMENT TO ARBITRATE” that “REQUIRES YOU TO SUBMIT CLAIMS YOU HAVE AGAINST US TO BINDING AND FINAL ARBITRATION.”

The warning also mentions that a user can opt out within 30 days. Christensen didn’t opt out.

Christensen can appeal Whitehead’s order to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, and arbitration wouldn’t necessarily mean Christensen won’t obtain justice. She could win the arbitration, and StubHub could be ordered to pay at least the difference between what she paid ($14,000) and what she received ($3,600), meaning about $10,400.

But arbitration has several limitations. For one, as an arbitration proceeding, Christensen v. StubHub won’t be certified as a class action, which is a much more threatening form of litigation—with potential massive damages—than a single-plaintiff suit. There also won’t be a jury, who might be more empathetic to Christensen’s situation than an arbitrator. 

Arbitration is also conducted in private, with parties bound by confidentiality. In litigation, the dispute is played out in a public forum that interested groups can follow. A company may be more inclined to try to settle a litigation than an arbitration because of the potential exposure.

Arbitration is also binding, with limited avenues to persuade a judge to vacate an arbitration award. A party could argue that an arbitrator refused to consider relevant evidence, failed to follow basic legal principles or undertook some other serious misstep that arbitrators seldom commit.

Arbitration provisions in sports tickets have also generated litigation. 

In 2021, an Illinois court allowed a fan who was hit in the face with a foul ball at Wrigley Field to sue the Chicago Cubs despite her ticket containing an arbitration clause. The court reasoned that the clause was in very small font, wasn’t in bold or all caps and the ticket only summarized the arbitration clause rather than offer its specifics.

Those lines of criticism do not appear applicable in StubHub’s tickets for Taylor Swift, as Judge Whitehead deemed the arbitration clause sufficiently noticeable and clear.
